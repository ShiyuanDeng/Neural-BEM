# Fully Modal Müller Inversion with a Wavefield-Resolved Atlas
## Research vision and theoretical foundations

**Project:** GPR / Neural-BEM  
**Date:** 29 September 2026  
**Status:** Research direction, not an executed method or an experiment specification.

> **Big picture.** Bring the boundary geometry, boundary wavefields, Müller operator, scattering response, and shape sensitivities into a compatible spectral description. Use an evolving atlas to understand which wavefield interactions carry information about each shape direction, which of those interactions the acquisition observes, how accurately they must be computed, and when that information can support useful finite reconstruction steps. The intended outcome is a principled connection between physical-frequency continuation, geometric complexity, update freedom, and wavefield resolution—not merely a smaller matrix or a larger heatmap.

## 1. The idea we are bringing together

The original atlas question was: **at the current reconstruction, how does each physical frequency act on each shape harmonic?** It included the direction of residual-dependent updates and the reinforcement, conflict, redundancy, and complementarity between frequencies. The modal extension asks a further question: **through which boundary-wavefield and incoming/outgoing interactions is that shape information generated and observed?**

This combines two lines of work that have been largely separate in the project. The Laurent line developed a coefficient-native Müller representation and differentiable scattering responses. The continuation line developed trajectory-dependent shape sensitivities and tested whether they improve reconstruction decisions. The proposed synthesis makes the wavefield structure itself part of the explanation of the inverse, instead of treating the wavefield only as an intermediate quantity used to assemble a Jacobian. ([P1], [P3], [P4])

The literature supports the ingredients, but not an automatic benefit from their combination. Trigonometric Galerkin dielectric solvers establish the forward foundation; scattering-coefficient analysis connects shape harmonics to angular scattering channels; information-optimal illumination connects response derivatives to measurement design; and adaptive inverse-discretization theory connects numerical accuracy to inverse requirements. The proposed contribution is the **connection between these structures along evolving, noncircular transmission reconstructions**. ([R1], [R4], [R5], [R7], [R10])

A useful organizing distinction is:

**Information generated by the object → information accessible to the acquisition → information computed reliably → information usable in a finite update.**

These are not equivalent. The atlas should help distinguish them rather than compress them prematurely into one score.

## 2. What the existing work establishes—and what it does not

The repository records a working experimental node-free Laurent/Fourier Müller pipeline, including coefficient assembly, acquisition, derivatives, and scattering-matrix compilation. Its demonstrated inverse scope is narrower than the later unrestricted non-star-shaped continuation studies, and its direct assembly was not a competitive general replacement for the accuracy-matched nodal reference. This is evidence for feasibility and reusable mathematical machinery, not an established fully modal atlas inverse. [P1]

The modal-compression closeout found useful structure but no noncircular case satisfying its selected common forward/derivative mask gate. It explicitly did **not** prove that all compression, all derivative strategies, or all modal analysis must fail. Its principal question was computational economy, whereas the renewed question also concerns physical interpretation and decision value. A dense modal operator can still contain interpretable symmetry, correlations, cancellation, and resonant structure. ([P2]; [R8])

The atlas studies also impose a constraint on the new research claim. SC-043's prospective band selector did not outperform fixed release and stagnation controls despite qualified diagnostics. Therefore, additional modal detail cannot be assumed to improve continuation merely because it is more informative descriptively. It must explain or predict something relevant that the previous decision rule missed. [P4]

The earlier project reports recommended derivative-aware modal compression and warned about coefficient-only bandwidth growth. Those recommendations remain historical motivation, not evidence that the new combined method already succeeds. This document broadens the purpose from **compressing modal interactions** to **understanding and using modal shape information**, while retaining the reports' numerical cautions. (D1, §§“Main direction” and “Novelty gaps”; D2, pp. 8–12)

## 3. Fixed terminology: the four controls and the modal indices

The missing “fourth frequency” is most naturally the **boundary-trace harmonic order**, with cutoff **$K_u$**. It is a spatial oscillation along the boundary, not an additional temporal frequency.

| Symbol | Meaning | Role |
|---|---|---|
| $f$, $\omega=2\pi f$ | Physical temporal frequency | Changes the physical scattering problem. |
| $k_e(f),k_i(f)$ | Exterior/interior wavenumbers | Include the medium properties; not interchangeable with frequency in GHz. |
| $K$ | Stored Cartesian boundary half-bandwidth | Controls the available finite shape representation. |
| $M$ | Scalar normal-update half-bandwidth | Controls the local deformation space. |
| $K_u$ | Boundary-trace half-bandwidth | Controls numerical resolution of the boundary wavefields. |
| $P$ | Diagnostic normal-harmonic cutoff | May exceed the active update band $M$. |
| $p$ | Shape-direction index | Labels a stated deformation basis, not automatically a Cartesian state coefficient. |
| $i,j,n$ | Internal boundary-wavefield Fourier orders | Label trace coefficients and modal equation coefficients. |
| $a,b$ | External incident/outgoing modal indices | Label physical field channels, not internal Müller unknowns. |
| $N$ | Boundary sampling/quadrature count | Belongs to a sampled implementation or reference calculation. |
| $B_{\mathrm{work}}$ | Intermediate coefficient-workspace cutoff | Controls coefficient-algebra truncation separately from $K_u$. |

The distinction between geometry, updates, and traces is essential. A low-degree circle may support rapidly varying fields at high physical frequency. A high-degree geometric representation may contain directions the data cannot distinguish. Smooth-boundary spectral approximation and inverse-scattering regularization address different errors. ([R1], [R2], [R3]; D3, pp. 17–20)

For one interface, using the same cutoff for two traces gives

$$
u_D(t)=\sum_{|n|\le K_u}d_n e^{\mathrm i n t},\qquad
q(t)=|\gamma'(t)|\,\partial_\nu u(t)
     =\sum_{|n|\le K_u}q_n e^{\mathrm i n t},
$$

and hence **$2(2K_u+1)$ complex unknowns**. This is dimension counting, not a statement about the minimum sufficient cutoff. The archived modal track used $M$ differently in some records; throughout this document, $M$ means only the normal-update band. [P1]

At the algorithmic level, the intended continuation state is

$$
\bigl(\mathcal F_\ell,K_\ell,M_\ell,K_{u,\ell}\bigr),
$$

where $\mathcal F_\ell$ is the active frequency set and $\ell$ the reconstruction stage. The four controls need not increase together. Their dependencies are a subject of the research, not a prescribed diagonal ladder.

## 4. What “fully modal” means in this vision

The intended architecture is coefficient-native, not just a nodal solver with Fourier plots attached:

```text
Cartesian Fourier/Laurent shape coefficients
                  ↓
       modal Müller transmission operator
                  ↓
        forward and reciprocal trace coefficients
                  ↓
  scattering response and complete shape derivatives
                  ↓
       wavefield-resolved, trajectory-dependent atlas
                  ↓
 decisions about physical data, shape freedom, and numerical fidelity
```

Three claims must nevertheless remain separate. A **modal formulation** uses spectral trial/test spaces and modal unknowns. A **coefficient-native implementation** performs significant geometry/operator/derivative work directly in those coefficients. A **strictly node-free implementation** additionally avoids physical boundary samples in the specified operations. A hybrid Galerkin assembler can be genuinely modal while sampling its smooth kernel coefficients. A coefficient-native solver still has truncation errors and is not literally discretization-free. ([R1], [R2]; D1, pp. 6–7)

The long-term ambition is to make modal structure computationally useful throughout the inverse. Independent nodal evaluation remains valuable as a reference; its availability does not weaken that ambition. Conversely, eliminating every sample is not by itself evidence of better reconstruction or more information.

### 4.1 Mathematical setting

The immediate setting is a smooth, fixed-topology, two-dimensional scalar transmission problem motivated by TMz dielectric scattering. With time dependence $e^{-\mathrm i\omega\tau}$, equal permeability, and fixed material parameters,

$$
(\Delta+k_j^2)u=0\quad\text{in each homogeneous region},
\qquad [u]=[\partial_\nu u]=0,
$$

with outgoing radiation in the exterior. The initial theoretical discussion concerns the full-space model. Layered ground, finite antennas, loss, unknown materials, or topology change require their own extensions; they are not obtained merely by changing basis. ([R3], §2; [R4], §2; [P1])

The state curve is

$$
\gamma(t)=\sum_{r=-K}^{K}c_r e^{\mathrm i r t},\qquad 0\le t<2\pi.
$$

This is a Cartesian complex curve, not a radial graph and not automatically a univalent conformal map. Regularity and non-self-intersection remain independent requirements. Reparametrization changes its coefficient spectrum; therefore neither $K$ nor a coefficient-tail statistic is intrinsically a curvature bound. (D3, pp. 3–6)

### 4.2 Singular structure is analytically known; geometry mixes modes

A representative pulled-back kernel has the form

$$
\mathcal K_f(t,t')=
\alpha_f(t,t')\,\log\!\left(4\sin^2\frac{t-t'}2\right)
+\beta_f(t,t').
$$

The universal logarithmic factor has Fourier coefficients

$$
\widehat\ell_0=0,\qquad \widehat\ell_n=-\frac1{|n|}\quad(n\ne0).
$$

This permits exact treatment of that singular factor; it does not make the entire Helmholtz operator exact at finite bandwidth. Fourier differentiation becomes multiplication by mode number, and multiplication becomes convolution. Regularized hypersingular operators can similarly be expressed through weaker singularities and surface derivatives. These are established spectral and shape-calculus ingredients, not proposed novelties. ([R1], [R2], [R6])

Analyticity of a periodic smooth factor in a complex strip gives exponentially decaying Fourier coefficients by contour shifting. Finite smoothness gives algebraic decay instead. This explains why modal approximation can be efficient, but it does **not** establish that the complete evolving Müller matrix and all its shape derivatives share a small sparse pattern. Cai–Xu prove compression results for a particular operator class; transferring them requires matching their hypotheses. [R9]

## 5. The atlas has three connected levels

### 5.1 Internal operator atlas

Let $A_f(\Gamma)$ be the modal Müller system, with the two trace-block labels suppressed. Then

$$
\mathcal D_{f,p,i,j}=D_p(A_f)_{ij}
$$

asks: **how does shape direction $p$ alter the coupling from internal trace order $j$ into equation order $i$, at frequency $f$?**

This is the literal four-index extension suggested by the modal matrix. The current geometry, acquisition context, coordinate choice, and numerical cutoffs accompany it as conditioning information. It is not a universal geometry-independent lookup table.

The internal atlas is useful for studying operator structure and numerical sensitivity. It is not, by itself, a map of measured information: a strong internal coupling may be weakly excited or weakly observed. The response derivative below states the distinction algebraically.

### 5.2 Physical response atlas

For a fixed external basis, let $B_f$ inject incident modal fields, $X_f$ contain their solved boundary traces, and $R_f$ extract outgoing coefficients:

$$
A_fX_f=B_f,\qquad T_f=R_fX_f.
$$

Here $T_f$ is the regular-incident-to-outgoing transition response, not necessarily a unitary scattering matrix. Differentiating this identity gives

$$
D_pT_f=(D_pR_f)X_f+
R_fA_f^{-1}\bigl[(D_pB_f)-(D_pA_f)X_f\bigr].
$$

This **direct chain-rule consequence** makes clear why a heatmap of $D_pA_f$ alone is insufficient. The observed effect also depends on illumination, solved fields, propagation through the inverse operator, and readout. Shape differentiability provides the underlying analytical justification; the project's compiled-response work supplies an existing computational realization. ([R6]; [P1])

The external tensor

$$
\boxed{\mathcal S_{f,p,b,a}=D_p(T_f)_{ba}}
$$

has the intended interpretation:

> **At physical frequency $f$, how does shape direction $p$ change outgoing mode $b$ under incident mode $a$?**

A cylindrical expansion has a chosen origin and a domain of convergence. Its indices should not be confused with intrinsic boundary harmonics or used outside its valid source/receiver geometry. A physical-channel response can instead be used where a particular cylindrical representation is unsuitable. ([R4], §2; [P1])

### 5.3 Acquisition- and residual-aware atlas

For a fixed acquisition, write

$$
y_f=\mathcal O_f(T_f),\qquad
J_f[:,p]=\mathcal O_f(D_pT_f),
$$

where $\mathcal O_f$ includes actual illuminations, receiver readout, and channel selection. These expressions assume $\mathcal O_f$ is independent of shape; moving the expansion origin or basis introduces the corresponding additional derivative terms.

The atlas then retains not only sensitivities but their complex phases, residual alignment, and inter-direction correlations. With residual defined as prediction minus observation, a whitened Jacobian and residual, and objective $\Phi_f=\tfrac12\|r_f\|^2$, the gradient and Gauss–Newton matrix for real shape variables are

$$
g_f=\operatorname{Re}(J_f^*r_f),\qquad
G_f=\operatorname{Re}(J_f^*J_f).
$$

These are algebraic definitions for the stated least-squares objective, not interchangeable notions of observability. Noise whitening, objective weighting, and a physical metric on shape coefficients have different roles. In particular, Cartesian state coefficients and normal-update coefficients must be connected through their induced physical normal velocities; merely giving them the same index does not equate them. ([R3], §2.1; [R4], §4; D3, pp. 9–10)

## 6. The key analytical bridge: shape sensitivity is a coherent product of wavefields

For equal permeability, fixed wavenumbers, fixed point-source/receiver positions, and the outgoing Green function satisfying $(\Delta+k_e^2)G=-\delta$, the reciprocal transmission sensitivity, with wavenumber-squared jump $\Delta_f$, can be normalized as

$$
D F_f[h]_{rs}=\Delta_f\int_\Gamma u_{f,s}\,v_{f,r}\,h\,ds,
\qquad \Delta_f=k_i^2-k_e^2.
$$

Here $u_{f,s}$ is the total field due to source $s$, and $v_{f,r}$ is the corresponding reciprocal receiver field. Other measurement normalizations carry their known prefactors. This identity follows from differentiating the scalar contrast problem and using reciprocity; it is the identity used in the project. General unequal-permeability interfaces require additional terms. There is no conjugation between these two reciprocal fields; Hermitian conjugation enters the objective gradient later. ([R3], Theorem 2.1, equal-density specialization; [P1], [P3])

**Direct Fourier consequence.** On normalized arclength $\vartheta=2\pi s/L$, expand

$$
u_{f,s}=\sum_n U_{f,s,n}e^{\mathrm i n\vartheta},\quad
v_{f,r}=\sum_j V_{f,r,j}e^{\mathrm i j\vartheta},\quad
h=\sum_p\widehat h_p e^{\mathrm i p\vartheta}.
$$

Orthogonality gives

$$
\boxed{
D F_f[h]_{rs}
=\Delta_fL\sum_{n+j+p=0}
U_{f,s,n}V_{f,r,j}\widehat h_p.
}
$$

Thus the complexified sensitivity column for $e^{\mathrm i p\vartheta}$ is

$$
J_{f,rs,p}=\Delta_fL\sum_nU_{f,s,n}V_{f,r,-p-n}.
$$

Physical real cosine/sine columns combine the $p$ and $-p$ expressions. If a non-arclength parameter is used, the Fourier coefficients of $|\gamma'|$ participate in the convolution as well. For a projected finite-update construction, $h$ is its complete first-order normal velocity, not an assumed unprojected harmonic.

This decomposition exposes the **wavefield interactions contributing to an existing sensitivity**, not new independent data. Large terms may reinforce or cancel. A low-order shape sensitivity may depend on two high, nearly opposite field orders. Hence neither a field-energy plot nor a sum of squared modal contributions generally represents the full sensitivity:

$$
\left|\sum_i z_i\right|^2\ne\sum_i|z_i|^2.
$$

This is the main opportunity for additional analytics: explain a bright, dim, unstable, or conflicting atlas cell in terms of the fields that generate it.

A further exact truncation consequence is useful. If both traces are represented only through $K_u$, their product in this arclength formula contains no order beyond $2K_u$. A sensitivity calculation based solely on those truncated fields therefore cannot represent higher normal harmonics. This is **a limitation of that numerical approximation, not a physical resolution theorem**, and $K_u=M/2$ is not a general sufficiency rule because high opposing field orders can contribute to low shape orders.

## 7. What the literature suggests the modal atlas could explain

### 7.1 Harmonic signatures, symmetry breaking, and mode mixing

Ammari–Chow–Zou identify scattering coefficients with a two-angle Fourier representation of the far-field pattern. Their near-circle, weak-contrast analysis relates the response to a shape Fourier coefficient through products of Bessel functions. Theorem 2.4 imposes a specific small-contrast/small-deformation scaling, so its amplitude formula must not be exported unchanged to strongly noncircular, high-contrast objects. ([R4], Theorems 2.2 and 2.4)

Rotational symmetry supplies the simpler structural statement: about a circle, a real perturbation of order $p$ produces first-order external couplings with $b-a=\pm p$, in the stated incoming/outgoing convention. The radial factors depend on the physical transmission problem. Away from a circle, each external channel generally excites many boundary harmonics, so the simple channel signature mixes even though the Fourier-product identity in Section 6 remains valid. ([R4]; [P1])

**Research hypothesis:** the evolution of these interaction patterns may explain when distinct shape directions become confounded, or when a frequency contributes genuinely different information. This is more specific than expecting all matrices to be sparse.

Betcke–Phillips–Spence show that spectral behavior changes substantially away from the circle, including nonnormality and the relevance of scaled inner products. Their acoustic operator analysis supports looking beyond raw diagonal bands and eigenvalues; it is not a theorem about arbitrary Müller inverse trajectories. [R8]

### 7.2 Strong sensitivity and short finite validity can share a mechanism

Ammari–Chow–Zou's high-contrast work establishes resolution enhancement at particular resonant contrast values, rather than a monotonic benefit from larger contrast. Byrnes–Foreman analyze geometry/system perturbations through movement of scattering poles. Together they motivate distinguishing resonant information enhancement from easy nonlinear optimization. ([R11], [R12])

**Illustrative deduction, not a fitted law for this project.** Suppose one response component is dominated locally by a simple pole,

$$
T(f,\eta)\approx\frac{C}{f-f_*(\eta)},
$$

and neglect residue variation while linearizing the pole position. Then

$$
\partial_\eta T\approx
\frac{C\,\partial_\eta f_*}{(f-f_*)^2},
\qquad
\frac{|\delta\eta\,\partial_\eta f_*|}{|f-f_*|}\ll1
$$

is the natural smallness condition for the first-order pole expansion. Approaching the pole can simultaneously increase sensitivity and reduce the valid shape-step range. This offers a physically motivated explanation to investigate, not a diagnosis already established for the failed kite, C, or high-contrast paths.

A Fourier harmonic is not itself a resonant eigenmode; resonant states are combinations of spatial harmonics. Wigner–Smith interpretations also require care: Hermitian time-delay statements concern an appropriately normalized, complete, unitary scattering matrix at real frequency, not an arbitrary regular-to-outgoing $T$ or a paired-data submatrix. [R12]

### 7.3 Informative illuminations need not be the strongest-scattering illuminations

Bouchet–Rotter–Mosk connect derivatives of the scattering response to maximum-information coherent input states. Bouchet–Seifert–Mosk also optimize illumination for multiparameter diffractive estimation. These establish a direct precedent for using response sensitivities to make measurement choices, rather than merely explaining a plot. Their estimation settings are not our evolving boundary inverse. ([R5], [R15])

For a classical proper complex Gaussian measurement model with shape-independent noise covariance $C_n$, a known incident-amplitude vector $z$, and accessible response $H_f$, the Fisher matrix for real shape parameters is

$$
\mathcal I_{pq}(z)
=2\operatorname{Re}\left[
z^*(D_pH_f)^*C_n^{-1}(D_qH_f)z
\right].
$$

This is the Fisher identity for the stated statistical model. The input-power metric and realizable source set must also be specified. Optical photon-noise or intensity-only formulas are related precedents, not identical noise models.

The 2026 Verreussel–Seifert–Mosk preprint makes the overlap particularly close: it develops a multiparameter information tensor, optimality criteria, and nuisance-parameter treatments for coherent scattering, with coupled-dipole examples. Gutiérrez-Cuevas and colleagues use tensors for intensity-based wavefront shaping, where intensity's quadratic dependence on fields creates a different tensor structure. ([R7], §§2.2–2.5; [R13])

Feldman and colleagues' 2026 metasurface study connects sensitive resonant modes to information-optimal structured illumination. It supports the intuition that modal excitation and informative sensing can be analyzed together, but not a claim of demonstrated GPR continuation performance. ([R14], §§3–5)

**Research implication:** a wavefield-resolved atlas can organize accessible source combinations, output channels, and nuisance-confounded shape directions. That is a plausible extension, not novelty merely from introducing a tensor.

### 7.4 Finite shape behavior remains a separate theoretical layer

A modal Jacobian is still a first derivative. If $\mathcal T_{\Gamma,K}(\eta)$ denotes the actual finite-update construction, the relevant expansion is

$$
F_f(\mathcal T_{\Gamma,K}(\eta))-F_f(\Gamma)
=J_f\eta+\mathcal E_f(\eta).
$$

Both the scattering nonlinearity and the update construction affect $\mathcal E_f$. Borges–Rachh–Greengard's penetrable-object work highlights the difficulty of finite admissible-curve families. Costabel–Le Louër provide shape-differentiability foundations, and Bao and colleagues develop high-order shape-Taylor recurrences, including transmission conditions. None supplies a ready-made predictor of this project's accepted progress. ([R3], §5; [R6]; [R16])

An elementary least-squares identity makes the missing link precise. If the whitened finite remainder obeys $\|\mathcal E_f(\eta)\|\le\epsilon$, then

$$
\text{actual decrease}\ge
\text{predicted decrease}
-\|r_f+J_f\eta\|\,\epsilon-\tfrac12\epsilon^2.
$$

This follows by expanding the squared residual. It becomes a predictive certificate only with a useful prospective remainder bound; measuring the remainder after a trial is an a-posteriori check.

The proposed connection is to ask whether modal interaction or resonance structure explains the size and direction of that remainder. A superior local information map alone does not resolve the optimization problem.

## 8. How the fourth control can belong to continuation

Physical frequency, state band, update band, and trace band have different jobs. Changing $f$ changes the data/model physics. Changing $K$ changes the finite shape family or its representation. Changing $M$ changes the allowed local deformation space. Changing $K_u$ changes numerical fidelity. A coherent continuation method can coordinate them, but should not disguise forward underresolution as geometric regularization. ([R3]; [R10]; D3, pp. 17–20)

Theoretical support for inverse-aware numerical fidelity comes from Kaltenbacher–Kirchner–Veljović: adaptive discretization of regularized Gauss–Newton can be controlled through quantities of interest with convergence results under stated accuracy conditions. This is a rationale for letting the inverse determine required numerical accuracy, not an existing theorem prescribing a Müller cutoff. [R10]

**Elementary bound linking trace accuracy to shape sensitivity.** Let $\widetilde u,\widetilde v$ approximate the exact reciprocal traces on a fixed boundary. The identity in Section 6 and Cauchy–Schwarz give

$$
|D F[h]-\widetilde{D F}[h]|
\le |\Delta_f|\,\|h\|_\infty
\left(
\|u-\widetilde u\|_{L^2(\Gamma)}\|v\|_{L^2(\Gamma)}
+\|\widetilde u\|_{L^2(\Gamma)}\|v-\widetilde v\|_{L^2(\Gamma)}
\right).
$$

This is a direct inequality, not a new estimator. It shows why controlling both forward and reciprocal traces is relevant. A usable estimator still needs reliable trace-error information, and near a sensitive configuration its constants may be unfavorable.

There is also an important distinction between truncation and elimination. Partition a modal system into retained and omitted modes:

$$
\begin{bmatrix}A_{LL}&A_{LH}\\A_{HL}&A_{HH}\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}x_L\\x_H\end{bmatrix}
=\begin{bmatrix}b_L\\b_H\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Where $A_{HH}$ is invertible, exact elimination gives

$$
(A_{LL}-A_{LH}A_{HH}^{-1}A_{HL})x_L
=b_L-A_{LH}A_{HH}^{-1}b_H.
$$

This block-algebra identity explains why solving only $A_{LL}x_L=b_L$ is not generally equivalent to retaining the low modes of the exact solution. High modes can feed back into low ones. The atlas could help characterize that feedback, but a small high-mode field tail or a dark matrix entry alone does not certify it as negligible.

The envisioned numerical atlas therefore asks: **which trace resolution and interactions are needed to compute the present data and active shape sensitivities reliably?** The answer can be geometry-, frequency-, acquisition-, and direction-dependent. Compression or selective reuse is a possible consequence, not a prerequisite for useful analysis.

## 9. What must remain invariant, and what can legitimately change

### A change of basis does not create observations

For a fixed unitary transformation $Q$ of whitened measurement coordinates,

$$
\widetilde J=QJ,\quad\widetilde r=Qr
\quad\Rightarrow\quad
\widetilde J^*\widetilde J=J^*J,\qquad
\widetilde J^*\widetilde r=J^*r.
$$

This algebraic invariance explains the boundary of the claim. Modal analysis can reveal structure, support better decisions, or make calculations cheaper. It cannot increase information merely by relabeling the same data. For nonunitary transformations, the covariance and metrics must transform consistently. ([R4], §4.2)

Likewise, shape coordinates require a physical metric. A change from Cartesian coefficients to normal harmonics can preserve the same physical directions; truncating the two bases generally selects different subspaces. Apparent changes of conditioning or modal complexity must be separated from changes of scale, parametrization, and allowed deformations. ([R6]; D3, pp. 4 and 9–10)

### Simulated channels are not measured channels

A complete modeled $T$-matrix does not imply a complete measured multistatic response. Paired source–receiver acquisitions may not support arbitrary source superpositions and receiver combinations from the recorded data. Information calculations must use the available acquisition operator and its noise model. New measurements, coherent experimental control, post-processing of existing channels, and internally generated diagnostic fields are distinct resources. ([R4], §§2 and 4; [R5]; [R7])

Even with a fully measured response, recombining the same observations cannot create independent information. An optimally designed *new* experiment under a fixed power/time budget can improve estimation; the distinction is central when transferring wavefront-shaping results to GPR.

### Combining frequencies does not combine the physical solves

For independent frequency data, whitened likelihood contributions add, and so do their gradient and information contributions. Correlated errors require whitening the full stacked covariance. Every physical frequency still has its own transmission operator and solution. Adding different-frequency Müller matrices to replace their separate solves is not this method. ([R3], introduction and §2.1; D1, p. 8)

### Dense structure may still be useful

Operator sparsity, low-rank solution families, informative input states, and useful inverse directions are different structures. A failure of a common sparsity mask leaves the other questions open. Conversely, an attractive modal picture does not establish computational savings or improved reconstruction. ([R8], [R9]; [P2])

## 10. The research contribution we are seeking

The strongest formulation of the project is:

> **A fully modal description of penetrable scattering, coupled to a wavefield-resolved shape atlas that links physical frequency, finite geometric freedom, local update directions, and numerical wavefield resolution. Its purpose is to explain—and ultimately improve—how measured scattering information is converted into reliable finite shape evolution.**

There are three distinct possible contributions.

**An analytical contribution:** a quantitative explanation of shape signatures, mode mixing, cancellation, resonant sensitivity, or finite validity on evolving noncircular interfaces. Circle selection rules are a starting point, not the final result. ([R4], [R8], [R11], [R12])

**A numerical contribution:** an error-controlled coefficient-native Müller/derivative calculus in which trace bandwidth or retained interactions are chosen for inverse quantities of interest. The contribution would be the accuracy/cost mechanism and its scope, not Fourier Galerkin itself. ([R1], [R2], [R9], [R10], [R17])

**An inverse-method contribution:** decisions informed by the additional modal structure that improve accepted progress, reconstruction reliability, or accuracy at comparable total work over strong simple controls. This remains a hypothesis, especially after the negative SC-043 comparison. ([R3]; [P4])

The critical unanswered questions are whether modal structure explains useful finite behavior beyond existing aggregate Jacobians; whether that explanation persists after acquisition and noise restrictions; and whether the explanatory value can be converted into a decision or computational benefit without costing more than it saves. These questions define the direction without fixing an implementation, a threshold, or a sequence of experiments.

A node-free implementation is a legitimate long-term objective when it serves this calculus. It is not necessary for every early analytical observation, and its success should not be defined by absence of nodes alone. Similarly, adaptive-frequency selection, modal truncation, state regularization, localization, and topology management are different interventions; success in one should not be attributed to the others.

**In one sentence:** make the wavefield's modal structure part of our understanding of the inverse, and connect that understanding to the shape and frequency decisions the reconstruction actually needs to make.

Supplied full text. Relevant anchors: §2.1, equations (10)–(13), Theorem 2.1, and §5. Supports boundary/frequency continuation and the finite-geometry difficulty; its plane-wave acquisition and implementation are not identical to the project's paired near-field model.

**R4 — Ammari, H., Chow, Y. T., and Zou, J. (2016).** “Phased and Phaseless Domain Reconstructions in the Inverse Scattering Problem via Scattering Coefficients.” *SIAM Journal on Applied Mathematics*, **76**, 1000–1030. [DOI: 10.1137/15M1043959][R4]. Supplied full text. Theorem 2.2 connects scattering coefficients to angular Fourier data; Theorem 2.4 supplies the weak-contrast near-circle perturbation formula; §4 addresses phased reconstruction and noise. Its assumptions are retained in the discussion.

**R5 — Bouchet, D., Rotter, S., and Mosk, A. P. (2021).** “Maximum information states for coherent scattering measurements.” *Nature Physics*, **17**, 564–568. [DOI: 10.1038/s41567-020-01137-4][R5]. Publisher abstract verified. A response-derivative/information-operator precedent, not boundary continuation. The [2021 correction](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41567-021-01304-1) changes supplementary intermediate expressions; the publisher states that the final Fisher-information expression was unaffected.

**R6 — Costabel, M., and Le Louër, F. (2012).** “Shape derivatives of boundary integral operators in electromagnetic scattering. Part I: Shape differentiability of pseudo-homogeneous boundary integral operators.” *Integral Equations and Operator Theory*, **72**, 509–535. [Author manuscript, arXiv:1105.2474][R6]. Author abstract verified: smooth shape differentiability and treatment of stronger singularities through weakly singular operators and surface differential operators. No specific derivative-compression theorem is inferred.

**R7 — Verreussel, B., Seifert, J., and Mosk, A. P. (2026).** “Multiparameter Maximum Information States for Coherent Diffraction Measurements.” [arXiv:2606.01445v2, 5 June 2026][R7]. Full text inspected, particularly §§2.2–2.5. Develops multiparameter information and nuisance treatments; numerical model is a 2D coupled-dipole system. Close conceptual prior art, not a demonstrated moving-interface continuation method.

**R8 — Betcke, T., Phillips, J., and Spence, E. A. (2014; online 2013).** “Spectral decompositions and nonnormality of boundary integral operators in acoustic scattering.” *IMA Journal of Numerical Analysis*, **34**, 700–731. [DOI: 10.1093/imanum/drt002][R8]. Publisher abstract verified. Relevant to circle/ellipse/general-boundary spectral structure and normalization; not a universal Müller sparsity result.

**R9 — Cai, H., and Xu, Y. (2008).** “A Fast Fourier–Galerkin Method for Solving Singular Boundary Integral Equations.” *SIAM Journal on Numerical Analysis*, **46**, 1965–1984. [DOI: 10.1137/070703478][R9]. Publisher abstract verified. Provides Fourier compression and convergence for a stated operator class. Its bounds are not claimed for all four Müller blocks and their shape derivatives.

**R10 — Kaltenbacher, B., Kirchner, A., and Veljović, S. (2014).** “Goal oriented adaptivity in the IRGNM for parameter identification in PDEs: I. reduced formulation.” *Inverse Problems*, **30**, 045001. [DOI: 10.1088/0266-5611/30/4/045001][R10]; [author manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/1309.6800). Author abstract verified: adaptive discretization, quantities-of-interest accuracy conditions, and regularized Gauss–Newton convergence. Supports inverse-aware fidelity, not a ready-made trace-band rule.

**R11 — Ammari, H., Chow, Y. T., and Zou, J. (2018).** “Super-resolution in imaging high contrast targets from the perspective of scattering coefficients.” *Journal de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées*, **111**, 191–226. [DOI: 10.1016/j.matpur.2017.09.008][R11]. Publisher abstract verified: resolution enhancement at specific high-contrast values. The detailed theorems were not independently rederived here.

**R12 — Byrnes, N., and Foreman, M. R. (2025).** “Perturbing scattering resonances in non-Hermitian systems: A generalized Wigner–Smith operator formulation.” *Newton*, **1**(7), 100194. [DOI: 10.1016/j.newton.2025.100194][R12]. Publisher accessible text inspected for pole-shift theory and normalization assumptions. Numerical examples concern photonic networks, not the present inverse geometries.

**R13 — Gutiérrez-Cuevas, R., Bouchet, D., de Rosny, J., and Popoff, S. M. (2024).** “Reaching the precision limit with tensor-based wavefront shaping.” *Nature Communications*, **15**, 6319. [DOI: 10.1038/s41467-024-50513-8][R13]. Full text inspected. Intensity-based input/output optimization is a related tensor construction, not the same linear complex-field measurement model.

**R14 — Feldman, N., de Gaay Fortman, N., den Boef, A. J., Amitonova, L. V., and Koenderink, A. F. (2026).** “Wavefront Selective Modal Excitations for Optimally Informative Sensing in Fano-Resonant Metasurfaces.” *Nanophotonics*, **15**(9), e70119. [DOI: 10.1002/nap2.70119][R14]. Publisher text inspected, especially §§3–5. A theoretical/numerical resonant-metasurface sensing precedent, not experimental GPR evidence.

**R15 — Bouchet, D., Seifert, J., and Mosk, A. P. (2021).** “Optimizing illumination for precise multi-parameter estimations in coherent diffractive imaging.” *Optics Letters*, **46**, 254–257. [DOI: 10.1364/OL.411339][R15]; [author manuscript](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.03306). Author abstract verified. Establishes that multiparameter illumination optimization predates the 2026 preprint.

**R16 — Bao, G., Ma, H., Lai, J., and Li, J. (2026).** “Shape Taylor Expansion for Wave Scattering Problems.” *SIAM Journal on Applied Mathematics*, **86**, 482–505. [DOI: 10.1137/25M1723645][R16]. Publisher abstract verified: high-order recurrence formulas covering several acoustic/electromagnetic boundary conditions, including transmission. Used as a foundation for studying finite remainders, not as an existing atlas acceptance certificate.

**R17 — Pinto, J., Aylwin, R., and Jerez-Hanckes, C. (2021).** “Fast solver for quasi-periodic 2D-Helmholtz scattering in layered media.” *ESAIM: Mathematical Modelling and Numerical Analysis*, **55**, 2445–2472. [DOI: 10.1051/m2an/2021053][R17]. Journal abstract verified: spectral Galerkin transmission convergence under exclusions including Rayleigh–Wood frequencies. The periodic layered setting differs from closed inclusions.
